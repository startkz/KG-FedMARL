\section{RELATED WORK}
\label{sec-related-work}
The literature relevant to this work spans three areas: recovery and resilient control under cyber-physical attacks, semantic knowledge representation for CPS operation, and poisoning attacks against knowledge-based learning. Existing studies provide important components of the recovery loop, but they largely address these components separately. Our focus is narrower: the integrity of the runtime knowledge used to authorize recovery actions and to qualify subsequent online-learning updates.

\subsection{Recovery and Resilient Control under Cyber-Physical Attacks}
CPS recovery aims to return a compromised system to a target physical condition after an attack. Lu \emph{et al.} survey this area and distinguish recovery mechanisms according to whether a dedicated recovery controller is used, while also identifying diagnosis, attack history, and recovery-module assumptions as important open issues \cite{lu2024recovery}. Representative methods include real-time attack recovery based on linearized dynamics \cite{zhang2020real}, learning-assisted recovery from sensor attacks \cite{akowuah2021recovery}, and reinforcement-learning-assisted recovery of autonomous platforms \cite{fei2020learn}. Cross-layer resilience research further studies how cyber, communication, control, and physical dependencies shape defense and recovery decisions \cite{zhu2020cross}. Recent resilient-control work considers hybrid DoS/FDI attacks and multilayer coordination \cite{game2026hybrid,liao2025surveyresilient}, while power-distribution restoration has begun to coordinate communication recovery with physical reconfiguration \cite{zhou2025faultresilient}.

These studies establish the need for attack-aware recovery, but their main objects of analysis are physical states, control inputs, communication availability, or attack estimates. Runtime recovery knowledge is generally consumed as measurements, diagnosis outputs, estimated states, or predefined cyber-physical constraints. Once these inputs are provided to the recovery module, their integrity is not usually modeled as a separate state whose adversarial distortion, correction time, and downstream control influence are explicitly bounded. This distinction motivates the knowledge-level threat model studied here.

\subsection{Dynamic Knowledge Representation for CPS Operation}
Knowledge graphs provide a structured representation for heterogeneous entities, relations, context, and rules, with support for querying, validation, inference, and graph-based learning \cite{hogan2021knowledgegraphs}. Research on the interaction between KGs and machine learning has likewise emphasized both knowledge-enhanced learning and learning-assisted knowledge refinement \cite{damato2023machine}. For CPSs, recent work has moved beyond static asset graphs. Gill \emph{et al.} represent time-continuous system behavior directly in KGs \cite{liu2025timecontinuous}; Ji \emph{et al.} use weighted KGs to combine formalized engineering knowledge with data-derived information dependencies \cite{wang2025weightedkg}; and Banerjee and Choppella use ontologies to support dynamic control-software reconfiguration \cite{knowledge2025dynamic}. In critical power infrastructure, Wu \emph{et al.} construct a heterogeneous cross-domain KG for threat modeling \cite{wu2026multidimensional}. More recently, Sankaranarayanan \emph{et al.} integrate cyber and physical grid information through a unified ontology-driven KG and demonstrate real-time cross-domain queries under structural updates \cite{sankaranarayanan2026ontology}.

These results show that operational KGs can evolve with system state and can expose cyber-physical dependencies to automated reasoning. They do not, however, make graph evolution equivalent to trustworthy recovery knowledge. A runtime assertion may be semantically well formed and still be false because the evidence that created it was fabricated, replayed, delayed, correlated through a compromised upstream source, or selectively suppressed. Existing CPS-KG work primarily addresses representation, interoperability, reasoning, or reconfiguration; it does not provide a bound on how much an adversarial evidence source may change a recovery belief, nor does it define when such a belief is sufficient to authorize a field action.

\subsection{Knowledge Poisoning and Knowledge-Guided Learning}
The security of knowledge representations has been studied mainly from the perspective of knowledge-graph embeddings. Zhang \emph{et al.} show that targeted additions and deletions of triples can manipulate KGE predictions \cite{zhang2019kgpoison}; Bhardwaj \emph{et al.} exploit relation-inference patterns to poison link-prediction models \cite{bhardwaj2021kgpoison}; and Zhou \emph{et al.} demonstrate poisoning of federated KGE through malicious local updates without directly modifying a victim's private KG \cite{zhou2024fkgepoison}. These works establish that knowledge-based models are vulnerable to adversarial data, but their security objective is the integrity of embedding or link-prediction outputs. They do not consider a time-varying operational KG whose assertions are repeatedly reconstructed from heterogeneous field evidence and then converted into physical action authority.

A separate line combines KGs with learning and control. The broader KG--ML literature treats symbolic knowledge as a source of constraints or context for data-driven models \cite{damato2023machine}, and recent industrial work combines KG retrieval with game-theoretic multi-agent learning for robust control design \cite{rodrigues2026agentic}. Such architectures demonstrate the value of semantic knowledge for decision making, but they do not address a second propagation path important in online recovery: a transition collected under contaminated knowledge may remain in replay and affect the deployed policy after the original evidence has expired. Preventing this persistence requires explicit qualification of recovery transitions and bounded policy promotion, rather than only robust policy optimization.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}
KG-FedMARL therefore does not claim novelty from constructing a dynamic KG, using MARL, applying federated learning, or placing a safety filter after a learned policy. Its contribution is the security boundary connecting these components. We model recovery knowledge as a versioned belief state inferred from potentially adversarial evidence, bound correlated evidence through provenance-aware influence limits, require uncertain prerequisites to pass a risk-aware authorization gate before exact physical verification, and prevent unverified recovery transitions from directly modifying the deployed policy. This yields an explicit chain from adversarial evidence to bounded belief distortion, bounded false authorization, finite post-attack correction, and bounded policy deviation. The distinction is important: prior recovery work protects the plant or controller, prior KG work represents or reasons over operational knowledge, and prior KG-poisoning work attacks learned representations; this paper instead studies how corrupted operational knowledge propagates through a closed cyber-physical recovery loop.
