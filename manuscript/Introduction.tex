\section{INTRODUCTION}
\IEEEPARstart{R}{estoring} a distribution grid after a cyber-physical disturbance is a sequential control problem with incomplete evidence. The operator must identify the damaged electrical region, recover enough communication reachability to issue commands, respect protection and topology constraints, and choose a sequence of actions that restores critical load without creating a second unsafe state. A command that is valid in a feeder model may be unusable if the relay path is unavailable; a measurement that supports reconfiguration may be stale or corrupted; and a switch operation that helps one area may violate radiality, voltage, or thermal limits elsewhere. Fig.~\ref{fig-scenario} summarizes the setting considered in this paper.

The cross-layer nature of this problem is well established in secure and resilient CPS theory. Zhu and Xu argue that resilience should be designed across cyber, communication, control, and physical layers because an adversary or fault can exploit their interdependence rather than any layer in isolation \cite{zhu2020cross}. Recent resilient-control studies make this idea mathematically precise through game-theoretic control, resilient observers, secure model predictive control, event-triggered mechanisms, sliding-mode designs, and distributed consensus under denial-of-service, deception, stealthy, and hybrid attacks \cite{game2026hybrid,resilientmpc2025dos,stochastic2025tube,sampled2025observer,resilientadaptive2025hybrid,fixedtime2025sliding,fullydistributed2025consensus,tsfuzzy2025dual}. Distribution-system restoration work further shows that physical reconfiguration and communication recovery must be coordinated when situational awareness is degraded \cite{zhou2025faultresilient}. These studies provide the resilience basis for this paper, but most of them still embed cross-layer knowledge inside task-specific equations. Relations such as a relay controlling a breaker, a communication route being required by a switch action, or a previous action failing under a similar cyber condition are rarely represented as objects that can be queried, checked, and updated during restoration.

Knowledge graphs provide a natural way to represent such objects. General KG research treats entities, relations, ontologies, rules, reasoning, embeddings, and graph learning as parts of the same representation stack \cite{hogan2021knowledgegraphs,reinanda2020knowledge}. Work on machine learning and KGs further argues for a two-way connection: symbolic knowledge can constrain and explain learning, while learning can complete or refine symbolic knowledge \cite{damato2023machine}. In power and industrial CPSs, KGs have been used to represent time-continuous behavior, information dependencies, cross-domain threats, causal attack evidence, and knowledge-driven reconfiguration \cite{liu2025timecontinuous,wang2025weightedkg,wu2026multidimensional,incremental2026causal,hybridtcdf2026,knowledge2025dynamic}. The gap is not whether a KG can store CPS facts. The open question is how the KG should participate in a closed restoration loop: how it defines the state seen by learning agents, how it carries safety constraints, and how it records the outcome of executed actions.

Learning-based restoration adds another difficulty. Switching devices, feeder controllers, microgrids, distributed energy resources, and communication-repair units act locally, but their actions couple through power flow, cyber reachability, and restoration priority. Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) is attractive for such distributed decision making, and graph neural networks can encode non-Euclidean power-system topology \cite{gnnpowersystems2024,graphzero2025,hybridkgdrl2025}. Yet direct MARL is not acceptable for power-CPS restoration unless exploration is bounded by operating rules. It also conflicts with data locality: substations and edge controllers may not be able to share raw disturbance traces because of privacy, security, or bandwidth constraints. A usable method therefore has to answer four questions at once: how evidence changes the graph state, what cross-layer state the agents learn from, what prevents an unsafe learned action from reaching the field, and how heterogeneous distributed controllers train when their action spaces and communication clocks differ.

This paper answers these questions with KG-FedMARL, a knowledge-guided federated MARL framework for resilient restoration of power CPSs. The central design choice is to make the dynamic KG the common substrate of restoration. It is not an auxiliary feature store placed before a neural policy. It is the semantic state abstraction used by the graph encoder, the rule and dependency base queried by the safety filter, and the memory updated after each executed restoration action. Under this view, restoration becomes a knowledge-conditioned constrained Markov game: each federated client owns local operational data and may host one or more restoration agents; agents observe local measurements together with a relation-aware embedding of the fault-affected task subgraph; they propose role-specific local actions under a training-time centralized critic; and only actions that pass graph-rule pruning, surrogate screening, and exact physical validation are executed.

The contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
	\item We develop a cross-layer restoration formulation grounded in secure and resilient CPS design \cite{zhu2020cross}. Physical availability, cyber reachability, information quality, and operating safety are modeled as coupled restoration variables rather than as separate post-processing annotations.
	\item We define a dynamic heterogeneous power-CPS KG for restoration. Evidence from faults, measurements, commands, and suspected attacks is mapped to timestamped graph operations that add state entities, revise relation confidence, deactivate confirmed unavailable relations, and update action-outcome memory.
	\item We formulate restoration as a knowledge-conditioned constrained Markov game. Federated clients are data-owning cyber-physical sites, while restoration agents are decision roles hosted inside those clients. This distinction makes heterogeneous action spaces explicit.
	\item We design a safety-aware KG-FedMARL framework in which candidate actions pass a three-level filter: KG-rule pruning, surrogate feasibility screening, and exact physical validation on a small remaining set.
	\item We specify a block-wise asynchronous training protocol for substations, feeders, microgrids, and edge controllers. Shared graph encoders are aggregated across compatible clients, role-specific heads are aggregated only within compatible action roles or distilled through task-subgraph probes, and stale updates are down-weighted according to policy delay and graph-state delay. This avoids assuming that heterogeneous nodes can be trained by ordinary FedAvg.
\end{itemize}
