\section{INTRODUCTION}
\IEEEPARstart{R}{estoring} a power cyber-physical system (PCPS) after a disruptive event requires more than identifying a feasible post-fault electrical topology. Recovery decisions also depend on whether measurements are current and trustworthy, whether controllers and communication paths remain available, whether protection and authorization conditions are satisfied, and whether previous recovery outcomes remain valid under the present disturbance. A switching action that is electrically feasible may still be unavailable when its control path is lost; a measurement supporting reconfiguration may be delayed or corrupted; and an apparently successful action may later become misleading evidence if its outcome record has been manipulated. These dependencies make resilient restoration a sequential decision problem whose correctness depends not only on the physical state, but also on the integrity of the knowledge used to infer that state and authorize recovery actions. The need to reason across cyber, communication, control, and physical layers is well established in resilient CPS research \cite{zhu2020cross,liao2025surveyresilient}. Existing studies have developed game-theoretic resilient control, secure or resilient model predictive control, and observer/event-triggered mechanisms against denial-of-service, false-data-injection, and hybrid attacks \cite{game2026hybrid,resilientmpc2025dos,stochastic2025tube,sampled2025observer}. Representative power-system restoration work has also incorporated communication impairment and cross-layer operational constraints into reconfiguration and service-recovery models \cite{zhou2025faultresilient}. In the representative recovery and resilient-control formulations above, runtime inputs are modeled mainly as measurements, estimated states, attack estimates, communication states, or predefined constraints. The CPS-recovery literature also highlights assumptions on diagnosis, attack history, and the recovery module itself \cite{lu2024recovery}. These formulations leave the integrity of a reusable recovery-knowledge state less explicit as a separate security object.

Knowledge graphs (KGs) provide a useful representation layer for exposing cyber-physical dependencies as typed and queryable objects. General KG research supports heterogeneous entities and relations, schema and context, query and validation languages, and deductive or inductive graph learning \cite{hogan2021knowledgegraphs,reinanda2020knowledge}. Recent CPS work has used KGs to represent time-continuous behavior, weighted information dependencies, cross-domain threats, and knowledge-driven control reconfiguration \cite{liu2025timecontinuous,wang2025weightedkg,wu2026multidimensional,knowledge2025dynamic}. Related work has also explored incremental causal-graph learning and KG-assisted verification for online attack detection \cite{incremental2026causal,hybridtcdf2026}. These studies establish representation, reasoning, detection, or reconfiguration capabilities, but are not designed to bound how adversarial evidence changes a recovery belief and how that change propagates into action authorization and online learning. Under the threat model considered here, if corrupted evidence is accepted as ordinary graph state, an attacker need not corrupt the recovery policy directly; altering the knowledge from which action prerequisites and learning transitions are derived can already change downstream recovery decisions.

This paper studies this attack surface as \emph{recovery knowledge poisoning}. The central concern is the propagation path
\begin{align}
\label{eq-intro-propagation}
&\text{adversarial evidence}\rightarrow\text{recovery knowledge}\nonumber\\
&\qquad\rightarrow\text{action authority}\rightarrow\text{online policy}.
\end{align}
A fabricated controller-availability report, for example, may raise the belief that a remote switching action is executable; repeated poisoned outcomes may then enter replay memory and gradually bias the deployed policy even after the original report has disappeared. Anomaly detection, safety filtering, and federated learning address different parts of this pipeline; unless the trustworthiness of the information passed between them is explicitly modeled or verified, none by itself provides a bound on the complete knowledge-to-authorization-to-learning propagation path. The security objective is therefore not to assume that online knowledge is correct, but to ensure that bounded evidence compromise causes only bounded distortion of the recovery state and bounded influence on control and learning.

We address this problem with KG-FedMARL, a trustworthy online recovery framework that treats the recovery KG as a security-critical knowledge plane rather than as an auxiliary feature store. The framework separates a trusted semantic basis from dynamic runtime assertions whose validity must be inferred from evidence. Each assertion is maintained as a temporal belief with source identity and provenance. Evidence influence is calibrated, discounted with age, weighted by independently assessed source reliability, and capped at both the individual-report and provenance-group levels so that correlated or duplicated reports cannot accumulate unbounded authority. The resulting belief state is then used by a risk-aware recovery gate: evidence-dependent prerequisites must satisfy operation-specific confidence budgets, while invariant engineering constraints are checked independently by an authoritative physical verifier over a conservative state set. Learned surrogates may rank candidates but cannot authorize field execution.

The same trust boundary is extended to online learning. A transition collected during restoration is bound to the belief, deployed-policy, and verifier versions under which the action was authorized. New transitions first enter quarantine and can affect learning only after their prerequisite beliefs, provenance coverage, evidence completeness, and observed outcomes satisfy an explicit trust criterion. Policy adaptation is slower than belief updating and follows a stable--candidate separation: a candidate update is restricted by a trust-dependent KL radius, evaluated in shadow mode through the same recovery gate, and promoted only after safety, performance, and version-consistency checks. Federated training, heterogeneous action heads, and graph encoders may be used as deployment mechanisms, but they do not constitute the security root and cannot bypass local authorization or policy-promotion checks.

Formally, restoration is modeled as a constrained decentralized partially observable Markov decision process in which local decision information is augmented by task-relevant recovery beliefs. We distinguish invariant engineering constraints from evidence-dependent prerequisites and formulate four security objectives: bounded belief distortion under a bounded adversarial evidence budget, bounded false authorization of recovery actions, finite correction of poisoned beliefs after trustworthy evidence resumes, and bounded deviation of the deployed policy. The framework provides a corresponding mechanism for each objective. The analysis shows that provenance-group influence caps and temporal forgetting bound accumulated belief distortion; calibrated prerequisite budgets bound false-authorization risk; temporal forgetting yields a finite correction-time bound; and trust-gated policy promotion bounds cumulative deployed-policy drift. These guarantees are mechanism-level security properties and do not rely on claiming global convergence of the underlying nonlinear MARL optimizer.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Recovery-knowledge threat model and security formulation.} We formalize the recovery knowledge plane as an explicit attack surface and define recovery knowledge poisoning under bounded source compromise, evidence manipulation, replay, delay, and provenance correlation. The resulting formulation distinguishes trusted semantic invariants from uncertain runtime assertions and defines security objectives directly in terms of knowledge distortion, false action authorization, correction time, and policy deviation.

\item \emph{Trustworthy KG-FedMARL recovery framework.} We design three coupled mechanisms that control the propagation of erroneous knowledge: a provenance-aware temporal belief update with bounded group influence, a risk-aware recovery gate that separates uncertain semantic prerequisites from authoritative physical verification, and trust-gated online policy adaptation with quarantined replay and stable--candidate policy separation. These mechanisms are optimizer-agnostic and can be combined with decentralized MARL and federated deployment without making federation itself a security assumption.

\item \emph{Mechanism-level security guarantees.} We establish explicit bounds connecting the adversarial evidence budget and framework parameters to belief distortion, false-authorization probability, post-attack belief correction time, and cumulative deployed-policy drift. The analysis therefore characterizes how far corrupted recovery knowledge can propagate rather than only reporting end-to-end learning performance.
\end{itemize}
