\section{SYSTEM MODEL}
\label{sec-system-model}
This section defines the cross-layer power cyber-physical system (PCPS), the recovery knowledge plane, and the adversary considered in this work. Unlike conventional restoration models that treat diagnostic outputs and cyber-physical dependencies as correct side information, we explicitly model runtime recovery knowledge as incomplete and potentially adversarial. The central modeling distinction is between a trusted semantic basis and dynamic assertions whose validity must be inferred from time-varying evidence.

\subsection{Cross-Layer PCPS and Restoration Process}
\label{subsec-cross-layer-system}
We model a PCPS as an interdependent system
\begin{align}
\label{eq-cross-layer-system}
\mathfrak{S}_t=
\big\{\mathcal{G}^{p}_t,\mathcal{G}^{c}_t,\mathcal{D},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R}^{pc},\mathcal{C}^{\rm inv}\big\},
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{G}^{p}_t=(\mathcal{V}^{p}_t,\mathcal{L}^{p}_t)$ is the physical power network, $\mathcal{G}^{c}_t=(\mathcal{V}^{c}_t,\mathcal{L}^{c}_t)$ is the cyber communication and control network, $\mathcal{D}$ contains measurement, protection, control, and actuation devices, $\mathcal{U}$ denotes controllable restoration resources, and $\mathcal{C}^{\rm inv}$ denotes invariant engineering constraints that cannot be relaxed by runtime evidence.

The physical layer contains buses, feeders, transformers, breakers, distributed generators, storage units, loads, and microgrid interfaces. Its operation is constrained by power balance, voltage limits, thermal limits, device capacities, switching logic, radiality, and protection coordination. The cyber layer contains control centers, substation controllers, RTUs, IEDs, communication relays, edge servers, and monitoring terminals. Cyber links carry measurements, protection messages, control commands, and restoration coordination traffic.

The two layers are coupled by typed dependencies
\begin{align}
\label{eq-coupling-relation}
\mathcal{R}^{pc}=\{(u,r,v)\mid u,v\in\mathcal{V}^{p}_t\cup\mathcal{V}^{c}_t\},
\end{align}
including observation, actuation, communication, protection, and common-cause dependencies. For example, a bus-voltage observation depends on a sensor and its communication path; a remote switch operation depends on a controller, authorization rule, and command route; and a physical corridor failure may simultaneously disrupt an electrical line and a co-located communication link. These dependencies are decision relevant because an electrically feasible action may still be unavailable when its cyber prerequisites are not satisfied.

Let $x_t\in\mathcal{X}$ denote the latent cross-layer state. Under joint restoration action $\mathbf{a}_t$, the system evolves as
\begin{align}
\label{eq-system-transition}
x_{t+1}=f(x_t,\mathbf{a}_t,\xi_t),
\end{align}
where $\xi_t$ captures fault evolution, demand and renewable variation, communication delay, attack effects, and model uncertainty. Restoration actions include switching, feeder reconfiguration, load transfer, distributed-generation dispatch, storage support, microgrid islanding/reconnection, communication rerouting or repair, controller switching, and emergency shedding. The true state $x_t$ is not directly observable because the same disturbance that requires recovery may also corrupt or disable the sensing, communication, and control paths needed to diagnose it. Consequently, recovery decisions are made from local observations together with an online knowledge state inferred from heterogeneous evidence.

\subsection{Recovery Knowledge Plane and Adversarial Evidence}
\label{subsec-recovery-knowledge-plane}
We define the \emph{recovery knowledge plane} as the semantic state used to represent current cyber-physical availability, observation trust, action prerequisites, and restoration-relevant history. To separate stable engineering knowledge from attack-time observations, the recovery belief knowledge graph at time $t$ is represented as
\begin{align}
\label{eq-kg}
\mathcal{G}^{B}_t=
\big(\mathcal{T}^{0},\mathcal{A}_t,\mathcal{B}_t,\mathcal{P}_t,\mathcal{V}_t\big),
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{T}^{0}$ is the trusted ontology, schema, and invariant rule base; $\mathcal{A}_t$ is the set of runtime assertions; $\mathcal{B}_t$ contains beliefs over these assertions; $\mathcal{P}_t$ records evidence provenance and source dependencies; and $\mathcal{V}_t$ stores temporal validity information. The trusted layer $\mathcal{T}^{0}$ describes entity and relation types, authenticated rule semantics, and invariant engineering limits. Mutable runtime settings, device status, and attack-time availability remain in $\mathcal{A}_t$ and cannot be promoted to trusted invariants by online evidence alone.

For each dynamic assertion $q\in\mathcal{A}_t$, the system maintains a belief
\begin{align}
\label{eq-fact-belief}
b_t(q)=\Pr\big(q_t^{\star}=1\mid \mathcal{Z}_{0:t}\big),\qquad b_t(q)\in[0,1],
\end{align}
where $q_t^{\star}$ denotes the unknown ground-truth status of $q$ and $\mathcal{Z}_{0:t}$ is the evidence history available up to time $t$. Here $q$ is a Boolean or finite-valued operational predicate, or an interval assertion such as \emph{voltage-in-range}; raw continuous measurements remain evidence or physical-state variables rather than binary KG facts. This representation avoids equating missing evidence with failure or the absence of an alarm with trustworthiness. A dynamic assertion may therefore remain uncertain until sufficient independent evidence is available.

Evidence is collected from heterogeneous sources
\begin{align}
\mathcal{S}_{E}=
\mathcal{S}_{\rm meas}\cup
\mathcal{S}_{\rm prot}\cup
\mathcal{S}_{\rm IDS}\cup
\mathcal{S}_{\rm ctrl}\cup
\mathcal{S}_{\rm net}\cup
\mathcal{S}_{\rm out},
\end{align}
covering measurements, protection events, attack-detection outputs, controller logs, network status, and restoration outcomes. An evidence event is represented as
\begin{align}
\label{eq-evidence-event}
z_j=\big(q_j,v_j,s_j,t^{e}_j,t^{r}_j,c^{src}_j,p_j\big),
\end{align}
where $q_j$ is the assertion addressed by the evidence, $v_j$ is the reported value or event type, $s_j\in\mathcal{S}_E$ is the source identity, $t^{e}_j$ is the source-reported event time, $t^{r}_j$ is the receive time recorded by the trusted ingestion point, $c^{src}_j$ is the confidence reported by the source, and $p_j$ is its provenance chain. Source-reported confidence is treated as evidence metadata rather than as trust; a compromised but authenticated source may report an arbitrarily high confidence for a false statement.

The provenance component $\mathcal{P}_t=(\mathcal{V}^{P}_t,\mathcal{E}^{P}_t)$ captures dependencies among sensors, RTUs, gateways, detectors, and communication paths. This is necessary because apparently distinct reports may share the same compromised upstream component and hence cannot be treated as statistically independent votes. Provenance is therefore part of the security semantics of the knowledge state, rather than an audit-only attribute. The concrete belief-update rule, temporal decay, source-reliability estimation, and bounded provenance-group influence are introduced in Section~V.

\subsection{Threat Model and Trust Boundary}
\label{subsec-threat-model}
We consider an attacker whose objective is to corrupt the recovery knowledge plane so that incorrect beliefs alter restoration constraints or subsequent policy adaptation. The attacker may compromise a bounded subset of measurement devices, RTUs/IEDs, communication gateways, detector interfaces, controller telemetry/reporting interfaces, or outcome-reporting modules. Let
\begin{align}
\label{eq-attacker-sources}
\mathcal{S}_{A}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{E}
\end{align}
denote the controlled sources. For a compromised source, the attacker may fabricate, suppress, delay, replay, or modify evidence, manipulate the source-reported event time and confidence, and coordinate multiple compromised sources to create apparently consistent false evidence. Such operations induce an adversarial evidence stream
\begin{align}
\label{eq-poisoned-evidence}
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{0:t}=\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{Z}_{0:t}),
\end{align}
which can support false facts, suppress valid facts, replay stale availability, or poison restoration-outcome memory. We refer to these operations collectively as \emph{recovery knowledge poisoning}.

We adopt a strong-knowledge adversary: the attacker may know the system topology, ontology, recovery rules, evidence-scoring procedure, policy architecture, and public model parameters. Security therefore does not rely on algorithm secrecy. However, compromising one source does not allow the attacker to impersonate an uncompromised source. Source identities are authenticated, and the trusted ingestion point records $t^{r}_j$, so an attacker can replay an old authenticated message but cannot replace its provenance with that of an uncompromised source. The attack budget is characterized by
\begin{align}
\label{eq-attack-budget}
\Omega_A=\big(f_{p},B_{z},H_{A},\epsilon_{s}\big),
\end{align}
where $f_p$ bounds the number of compromised independent provenance groups, $B_z$ bounds manipulated evidence per decision window, $H_A$ is the attack duration, and $\epsilon_s$ bounds the magnitude of stealth-constrained continuous-data manipulation when applicable.

The trusted computing base consists of the authenticated and read-only semantic basis $\mathcal{T}^{0}$, the evidence-ingestion logic, the implementation of the belief-update mechanism, version management, the protected actuation path, the final physical safety verifier, and a certified fallback controller. These components may process adversarial inputs but are not assumed to be code-compromised. The runtime assertion layer $\mathcal{A}_t$ is explicitly untrusted until its beliefs are established from evidence. A learned surrogate feasibility model is non-authoritative: it may rank or reject candidates but cannot independently authorize field execution. The authoritative verifier evaluates invariant constraints over a conservative state set $\widehat{\mathcal X}_t$ that is assumed to contain the realized state, i.e., $x_t\in\widehat{\mathcal X}_t$. This state-set containment, together with correct verifier implementation, is the physical-safety assumption used later in the security analysis.

A compromised controller interface in our threat model may corrupt telemetry, status, or outcome evidence, but it cannot bypass the authenticated actuation path or the authoritative verifier to issue an unchecked field command. Direct takeover of the protected actuation path is outside the recovery-knowledge-poisoning guarantees and, when studied experimentally, is treated as an exogenous cyber-physical disturbance in $\xi_t$. The coordinating/federated server, when used, is likewise not part of the field-actuation trust root: it may coordinate model training but cannot bypass the local knowledge gate or the final physical verifier. Federated heterogeneity, shared encoders, personalized adapters, and aggregation rules are treated as implementation infrastructure rather than as the security contribution of this work.

Under this model, the attacker's ultimate objective is not merely to change a graph attribute. It is to create a chain of influence from adversarial evidence to an incorrect recovery belief, from that belief to an erroneous action constraint, and potentially from repeated erroneous evidence to an unsafe policy update. Section~IV formalizes the admissibility, recovery, and propagation-control objectives, while Section~V specifies the mechanisms used to bound this influence.
